## 4. Essential Grammar Deep Dive

The following patterns occur in our original practice material. They cannot yet be attributed to the song’s lyrics.

### Pattern 1: Express an intention with -(으)려고 하다 (-(eu)ryeogo hada)

**Grammar Formula:** Verb stem + **-(으)려고 하다 (-(eu)ryeogo hada)** = “to intend to” or “to be planning to.”

Use **-려고 (-ryeogo)** after a vowel or an ending consonant **ㄹ (rieul)**. Use **-으려고 (-euryeogo)** after other consonants, allowing for irregular verb changes. The polite conversational ending is **해요 (haeyo)**.

**How it works in the practice line:**

> 퇴사하려고 해요. — Toesaharyeogo haeyo. — I’m planning to resign.

Start with **퇴사하다 (toesahada, to resign)**. Remove the dictionary ending to get **퇴사하- (toesaha-)**, then add **-려고 해요 (-ryeogo haeyo)**. This presents the speaker’s intention; it does not say that the resignation has already happened.

**Everyday example 1**

- **Hangul**: 주말에 친구를 만나려고 해요.
- **Romanization**: Jumare chingureul mannaryeogo haeyo.
- **English Translation**: I’m planning to meet a friend this weekend.

**Everyday example 2**

- **Hangul**: 집에서 점심을 먹으려고 해요.
- **Romanization**: Jibeseo jeomsimeul meogeuryeogo haeyo.
- **English Translation**: I’m planning to eat lunch at home.

In the second example, **먹다 (meokda, to eat)** has a consonant-ending stem, so it takes **-으려고 (-euryeogo)**.

### Pattern 2: Express a wish with -고 싶다 (-go sipda)

**Grammar Formula:** Verb stem + **-고 싶다 (-go sipda)** = “to want to do something.”

Attach **-고 (-go)** directly to the verb stem. In polite conversation, the complete ending becomes **-고 싶어요 (-go sipeoyo)**. Use it for your own wishes in statements and to ask another person about theirs.

**How it works in the practice line:**

> 오늘은 좀 쉬고 싶어요. — Oneureun jom swigo sipeoyo. — I’d like to rest a little today.

**오늘은 (oneureun)** means “as for today”; **좀 (jom)** means “a little” and can soften the tone; **쉬고 싶어요 (swigo sipeoyo)** means “want to rest.” Korean naturally omits “I” when the speaker is clear.

**Everyday example 1**

- **Hangul**: 한국어를 배우고 싶어요.
- **Romanization**: Hangugeoreul baeugo sipeoyo.
- **English Translation**: I want to learn Korean.

**Everyday example 2**

- **Hangul**: 오늘 뭐 먹고 싶어요?
- **Romanization**: Oneul mwo meokgo sipeoyo?
- **English Translation**: What do you want to eat today?

**Meaning checkpoint:** Wanting to do something expresses a desire. Planning to do it expresses an intention. You can want a holiday before you have any concrete plan to take one.

## 5. Pronunciation Secrets (연음 & 받침)

**연음 (yeoneum)** means linking or liaison; **받침 (batchim)** refers to a syllable’s final consonant. These two examples come from the practice material rather than verified lyrics.

### 1. Link final consonants into vowel-initial endings

In **마음을 (maeumeul, heart/feelings + object marker)**, the final consonant of the noun links into the following vowel-initial particle. The spoken form is **[마으믈] (maeumeul)**. Keep the sound flowing instead of inserting a pause before the particle.

Similarly, **싶어요 (sipeoyo, want to)** is pronounced **[시퍼요] (sipeoyo)**. The final aspirated consonant links forward. Preserve its small puff of air.

### 2. Listen for consonant tightening

In **먹고 (meokgo, eat + connective ending)**, the spoken form is **[먹꼬] (meokkko, pronunciation rendering)**: the second consonant becomes tense after the preceding final stop. Say it with a firm, compact onset rather than an extra burst of breath.

Standard Revised Romanization remains **meokgo**, because this predictable tensification is not reflected in standard spelling-based romanization. Use the bracketed Hangul and audio to hear the distinction.

## 6. Cultural Context & Lyric Nuance

**퇴사 (toesa)** means leaving a company. It does not mean finishing your shift and heading home: that is **퇴근 (toegeun, leaving work for the day)**. This difference is essential when interpreting a workplace-themed title.

The word for resignation is ordinary workplace vocabulary, not inherently slang. In an informal caption or song title, however, announcing an intention to resign could express determination, frustration, humor, or imagined freedom. Those are possible readings; the supplied title alone does not establish the artist’s intended message.

The English phrase “I’m gonna” is conversational and informal. If “TOESA” represents the Korean noun discussed here, the title combines informal English with Korean workplace vocabulary. Full lyrics and context would be needed for a firmer interpretation.

In our original sentence, **마음 (maeum, heart/mind/feelings)** refers to someone’s inner feelings rather than the physical organ. **제 마음을 전하다 (je maeumeul jeonhada)** therefore means “to convey my feelings.” This is a useful expression when discussing gratitude, affection, or something personal.
